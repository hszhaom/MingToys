How to Share a Card

Open this page on desktop or mobile, take a screenshot of the card you want, and share it with the full guide link. The card is a conversation starter, not a final recommendation.

How the Scores Should Be Read

A high score means the breed is often easier to fit into that specific owner situation, not that the dog is automatically easy. For example, a Golden Retriever can score well for families and first-time owners while still needing daily exercise, brushing, training, and careful weight control. A French Bulldog can score well for apartment living while carrying serious heat and breathing considerations. The card is meant to slow down impulsive decisions, not replace the full guide.

The most useful score is usually the one that makes you uncomfortable. If a breed has a low apartment score, ask whether your building, neighbors, and schedule can handle the noise or exercise. If grooming effort is high, price local groomers before falling in love with the coat. If health risk is high, ask a veterinarian what routine care, emergency planning, insurance, and breed-specific screening may look like in your area.

Owner-Fit Signals Behind Each Card

Card Signal What It Includes How to Use It
Family Fit Temperament, sturdiness, patience, trainability, and supervision needs. Compare against your children's ages and household rules.
First-Time Fit Forgiveness, training difficulty, size, health risk, and management load. Choose the dog you can handle on a bad week, not only a good one.
Exercise Demand Walks, play, mental work, adolescence, and recovery after busy days. Match the score to your normal weekday routine.
Risk Level Strength, guarding instincts, public handling, heat risk, or health cost. Read the full guide before treating the breed as a casual choice.

Best Way to Use This Page

Pick three breeds that look realistic, then read their full guides back to back. Write down the hardest part of each one: fur, barking, vet cost, training, exercise, grooming, or public handling. The breed with the prettiest card is not always the best match. The better choice is the breed whose hardest part you are willing to manage for years.

Scores may also change by life stage. A puppy can be far more demanding than an adult dog of the same breed, and a senior dog may need different exercise, diet, and veterinary planning. Use the card as a first screen, then judge the individual dog in front of you.

PetStorie 1-to-5 Scoring Method

These are editorial planning levels, not survey results or veterinary risk predictions. PetStorie compares the breed profile, parent-club and health-screening context, and the ownership demands explained in the full guide. Scores use whole numbers so the cards do not imply precision the source material cannot support.

LevelFit MetricDemand or Risk Metric
1/5Poor match for this setting in most homes.Low routine demand or lower relative concern.
2/5Possible only with meaningful adjustments.Some extra planning beyond ordinary care.
3/5Conditional fit that depends on the household.Moderate time, cost, or management pressure.
4/5Often a workable fit for prepared owners.High demand that shapes the weekly routine.
5/5Strong fit when the individual dog also matches.Very high demand or concern requiring a firm plan.

Read the metric name before reading the number. A high Family Fit score is favorable. A high Health Risk, Exercise Demand, or Management Risk score warns that the owner carries more work or uncertainty. Individual dogs can fall outside the breed-level estimate because of age, line, health, training, and prior experience.
