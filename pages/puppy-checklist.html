Puppy Checklist

A puppy is easier to enjoy when the basics are ready before the first night. This checklist focuses on practical setup, gentle training, safe routines, and early veterinary planning.

Before the Puppy Comes Home

  • Choose a veterinarian and schedule the first visit.
  • Confirm the puppy's food, feeding schedule, vaccine record, and parasite history.
  • Set up a crate, pen, or puppy-safe room for rest and supervision breaks.
  • Remove electrical cords, toxic plants, small swallowable objects, and unsafe cleaners.
  • Buy bowls, leash, collar or harness, ID tag, bedding, chew toys, and cleaning supplies.

First Week Routine

  • Keep introductions calm and avoid overwhelming visitors.
  • Take the puppy out after waking, eating, playing, and before sleep.
  • Reward calm behavior, name response, gentle handling, and coming when called.
  • Use short training sessions instead of long, frustrating lessons.
  • Track appetite, stool, energy, coughing, vomiting, itching, or unusual behavior for the vet.

First-Night Setup

The first night is easier when the puppy has one boring, safe place to sleep. Put the crate or pen near enough that you can hear restlessness, but avoid turning every sound into playtime. Take the puppy out for a quiet toilet break, reward calmly, then return to the same sleep area. If the puppy cries, check for toilet needs or distress, then keep the routine gentle and predictable. The goal is not to force independence overnight; it is to teach that nighttime has a pattern.

Health Records to Collect

Record Why It Helps Who to Ask
Vaccine history Helps your veterinarian plan safe socialization and future shots. Breeder, rescue, shelter, or previous caregiver.
Food and feeding schedule Reduces stomach upset during the first transition. Current caregiver and your veterinarian.
Parasite prevention Clarifies deworming, flea, tick, and heartworm planning. Veterinarian or adoption organization.
Breed or family health notes Flags issues to discuss early, such as joints, breathing, eyes, or skin. Responsible breeder, rescue, and veterinarian.

What Not to Overbuy

Buy enough to start, but do not fill the house with gear before you know the puppy's habits. Many owners buy beds that get chewed, toys the puppy ignores, or harnesses that are outgrown quickly. Start with safe basics, then adjust after the first week. Spend more attention on supervision, routine, and veterinary planning than on decorative supplies. A simple setup with good habits is more useful than a large basket of products the puppy cannot safely use yet.

First-Week Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid giving the puppy the whole house too soon. Too much freedom often leads to toilet accidents, chewing, overtired biting, and confusion about where to settle. Keep the world small, supervised, and predictable while the puppy learns your routine. Also avoid crowded dog parks, harsh corrections, and sudden diet changes. If appetite, stool, breathing, energy, or behavior seems abnormal, call your veterinarian rather than waiting for the problem to become obvious.
